\section{The Solidity and Vyper Backends}
\label{sec:solidity}

The EVM backends translate the EventGraph into a deployable smart
contract.  Both languages target the same compilation pass via a
shared EVM intermediate representation (\TT{evm.EVMIR}); only the
final pretty-printer differs.  This section describes the actual
generated code rather than an idealised version, including the
assumptions its guarantees rest on.

\subsection{Threat model}
\label{sec:solidity:threats}

The compiled contract is meant to survive a transaction-ordering
adversary --- a builder or competing searcher who can choose which
pending transactions to include and in what order --- and to remain
liveness-preserving when a participant stops responding.  It is not
designed against:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \emph{Censorship.}  An adversary who excludes a participant's
  transactions past a deadline forces that participant to quit, which
  triggers the language-level quit handler.
\item \emph{Cross-contract interactions.}  The contract is a closed
  system; no calls into other contracts are emitted (the only
  outbound action is a direct ETH transfer to a registered
  participant address during withdrawal).
\item \emph{Gas-cost games.}  Gas is a real-world cost that affects
  which moves are rational, but the analytical backends ignore it.
  Bounded per-action gas envelopes (e.g.\ via
  GASTAP~\citep{Albert2020Gastap}) would be needed to bring gas into
  the strategic model; this is planned but not implemented.
\item \emph{Cryptographic primitives.}  The contract relies on
  keccak-256 being binding and hiding.  Domain separation and
  per-deployment binding are in the hash composition
  (\S\ref{sec:solidity:commit-reveal}), but the strength of the
  primitive itself is taken as given.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this section describes the construction.

\subsection{Storage layout}

The contract's storage is derived directly from the EventGraph.
There are five families of slots.

\paragraph{The schedule.}  The contract stores the EventGraph as a
table over a deterministic topological order of its nodes: each
node's owner and its predecessors (as a bitmask).  Per node it
records \TT{readyAt}, when the last predecessor resolved, and
\TT{resolvedAt}, when the node was played or resolved without a
value.  Per role it records \TT{quitAt}, the deadline the role missed,
if any; a flag \TT{aborted} records that some role never joined.  One
\TT{uint256 constant ACTION\_\textit{Role}\_\textit{n}} is
emitted per node, where \textit{n} is the node's stable identifier
(the phase index for nodes lifted directly from source, a fresh
counter for nodes introduced by the commit-reveal expansion ---
see \S\ref{sec:eventgraph}) and the constant's \emph{value} is
its topological index.  Per-action functions in the contract are
named \TT{move\_\textit{Role}\_\textit{topo}}, using the
topological index for uniqueness across the whole contract.

\paragraph{Role registry.}  A mapping
\TT{roles[address] => Role} associates EOAs with the roles they
have joined, and a per-role \TT{address\_\textit{Role}} slot
stores the registered address.  A small \TT{enum Role} carries one
constructor per role plus a \TT{None} sentinel.

\paragraph{Game state.}  For each \emph{written field} in the
EventGraph the contract stores the field's value.  A field with
\PUBLIC visibility uses a slot of the field's native type
(\TT{int256}, \TT{bool}, \ldots).  A field with \COMMIT
visibility uses two slots: a \TT{bytes32} commitment hash filled
in at commit time, and a separate native-type slot for the
cleartext value filled in at reveal time.  Each slot is
shadowed by a \TT{done\_\textit{slot}: bool} flag, so the
contract can distinguish ``never written'' from ``written to
the default value''.

\paragraph{Per-role bookkeeping.}  Two booleans per role:
\TT{done\_\textit{Role}} (the role has joined) and
\TT{claimed\_\textit{Role}} (the role has claimed its terminal
payout).

\paragraph{Game-wide constants.}  A constant \TT{TIMEOUT} sets the
inactivity window, in seconds (the default is 24 hours).  A
constant \TT{COMMIT\_TAG} (a hashed domain-separation tag) and the
contract's deployed address are folded into every commitment hash.

\subsection{Readiness, deadlines and quitting}
\label{sec:solidity:depends}

The contract has no global step counter.  A node is \emph{ready}
once every predecessor is resolved, and its readiness time is the
latest predecessor resolution (deployment, for a source node).  A
ready node resolves in one of three ways: its owner plays it; its
owner has already quit, and it resolves without a value; or its
deadline, \TT{TIMEOUT} seconds after readiness, passes, and its owner
quits at that deadline.  Quitting is persistent, as in the analysis
model.  Only a ready node can expire, so a player is never blamed for
a move it could not yet make; and a move is rejected once its own
deadline has passed, even if nobody has recorded the expiry yet.

Resolution is lazy.  An internal \TT{\_settle} walks the unresolved
nodes in topological order and resolves what it can; it runs at the
start of every move and withdrawal, and anyone may call it through a
public \TT{settle()}.  A per-node function checks only that its node
is ready and still open:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=sol]
function move_Guest_3(int256 _d) public {
    _beginMove(3, Role.Guest);  // settle; caller's role; ready; still open
    require(((_d >= 0) && (_d <= 2)), "domain");
    Guest_d = _d;
    done_Guest_d = true;
    _endMove(3);                // resolvedAt[3] = block.timestamp
}
\end{lstlisting}

Resolving an expired node at its deadline, rather than when someone
notices, keeps the timeline independent of who calls first: the next
node's deadline counts from the moment it could first be played.
Concurrent nodes share their predecessors and can be played in any
order; the openings of simultaneous moves are ordered, as described
in \S\ref{sec:commit-reveal}.

\subsection{Commits and reveals}
\label{sec:solidity:commit-reveal}

Every COMMIT-tagged write becomes a function that takes a
\TT{bytes32} commitment and stores it; every REVEAL-tagged write
takes the cleartext value plus a salt, recomputes the commitment,
and asserts equality with the stored hash before performing the
public write.  The commitment hash is composed as

\begin{equation*}
  \mathit{keccak256}\bigl(
    \mathrm{abi.encode}(
      \textsc{COMMIT\_TAG},\
      \mathrm{address(this)},\
      \mathrm{role},\
      \mathrm{actor},\
      \mathit{keccak256}(\mathrm{payload})
    )
  \bigr).
\end{equation*}

The pieces serve distinct roles.  The \texttt{COMMIT\_TAG} provides
cross-protocol domain separation.  \TT{address(this)} ties the
commitment to this particular deployment, preventing replay across
copies of the same protocol.  The \TT{role} identifier defends
against role-swapping replays within a single deployment; the
\TT{actor} address binds the commitment to the EOA that posted it.
The payload is pre-hashed before being folded in, so the outer
hash works over a fixed-size input regardless of the parameter
list.

Vyper emits the analogous construction using the language's
\TT{assert} idiom and slightly different storage syntax.  The
generated bytecode is comparable.

\subsection{Aborts, missing values and randomness}
\label{sec:solidity:bail}

\paragraph{Joining.}  Joining is a precondition of the game, not a
move: the analysis model offers no quit at a join.  If a role has not
joined by its deadline, the instance is \emph{aborted}: no further move
is accepted, and each role that did join withdraws its deposit.
Without this rule the remaining players would be paid from a pot that
was never filled.

\paragraph{Missing values.}  When a node resolves without a value, its
value slot and \TT{done\_} flag keep their defaults (zero and
\TT{false}).  Nothing is fabricated.  A field declared with an
\TT{or null} handler is typed \TT{opt T}, and the \TT{withdraw} clause
must inspect its \TT{done\_} flag.  A \TT{where} guard that reads
another role's field is discharged when that field is missing, the rule
VegasCore uses for guards whose required publications failed; the
analysis model applies the same rule.

\paragraph{Randomness.}  A \TT{sample} node has no entry point.  The
contract is deployed with a randomness beacon, and a draw is fixed by
the first beacon round published a fixed delay after its node becomes
ready.  The delay outlasts the chain's finality, so no proposer can see
the round while it can still decide whether the draw becomes ready.
Nobody chooses when it is drawn or what it is, and any later call
settles it.  The beacon must also keep each round secret until it
publishes it: a threshold beacon such as drand does, while a VRF
operator computes each output first and is trusted not to withhold one.
Block-derived randomness is not an option: whoever triggers a draw
that reads \TT{block.prevrandao} can do so from a contract that reverts
unless the outcome suits it, and retry in the next block.

The fair-play guarantee one would like --- ``every role can drive the
contract from any reachable configuration to a terminal allocation''
--- follows for protocols whose \TT{withdraw} clause is total on the
public state: every node resolves by its deadline, and settlement then
pays each role, including roles that quit.  It is not machine-checked.
The Ethereum test suite replays model traces on chain, including traces
in which players quit (played as silence past the deadline) and public
draws (played through a test beacon), and compares every payout with the
model's.

\subsection{Withdraw and payout}

The terminal \TT{withdraw} clause is compiled into one entry
point per role.  Each role calls its own
\TT{withdraw\_\textit{Role}()} to receive their share, guarded by
a \TT{claimed\_\textit{Role}} flag to prevent double-payment.
Withdrawal requires every node to be resolved (or the instance to be
aborted, in which case it refunds the deposit); a role that quit
still receives what the \TT{withdraw} clause assigns it.  The
payout expression is lowered to EVM IR by direct structural
translation; the body computes the expression against the public
state (including \TT{done\_} flag inspection for optional fields)
and transfers the resulting amount to the role's stored address
using a low-level \TT{call} with the computed value.

Conservation --- that role payouts plus burn sum to the pool on
every reachable branch --- is enforced statically at compile
time by exhaustive enumeration of the strategic tree
(\S\ref{sec:language}); any under- or over-payment is reported
before code generation.  A symbolic SMT-based replacement that
does not depend on Gambit-side enumeration is the natural next
step for games whose state space the enumerator cannot cover
(unbounded \TT{int} parameters, very deep trees); the current
check is best-effort on those and tracked in \file{TODO.txt}.

\subsection{Terminal audit}
\label{sec:solidity:audit}

A contract can enforce who moves, when, and with which values, but not
what players broadcast.  A pending or reverted transaction publishes
its calldata, so a player can disclose a hidden choice before the game
allows it; the opponent reads it and reacts.  In a coordination game
this lets a player move first and make the other follow, a deviation
the source game does not have.  With \TT{--audited}, the Solidity
backend emits the terminal-audit service of VegasCore's
sequential-equilibrium theorem.  Every role posts a bond of its payout
range (over every way the game can end, including quits and aborts)
divided by the claimed collection probability.  Events are granted one
at a time, in schedule order.  Every move carries the hash of the block
in which its node became ready, so a signed move proves it was made
once its node was granted.  A role that lets its own commitment expire
loses its bond; withholding an opening costs nothing.  After play ends,
anyone can submit a transaction signed by a game account, and the
contract judges it by its content in the phase it names.  A move of the
signer's own node, carrying that node's readiness hash and passing the
move's domain, guard and opening checks, is permitted whether or not it
was included in time; so are \TT{settle()} and the signer's own
withdrawal.  Anything else burns the bond: an early or wrong opening, a
move of another role's node, appended calldata, a transaction to
another address, and any blob or set-code transaction, whose side data
could carry a secret.  A watcher (\TT{vegas watch}) collects the game
accounts' transactions from the chain and from the pools of the nodes
it watches.  It is trusted for coverage only: it cannot frame a player,
whose signature, readiness context and content are checked on chain.
Coverage of traffic that never reaches a watched pool, protected
inclusion, finality, and the fee fields every sender chooses freely
remain assumptions; \file{docs/AUDITED-RUNTIME.md} lists them.
Public draws read the first beacon round after readiness plus a delay
longer than the chain's finality, so no proposer can see the round
while it can still decide whether the draw becomes ready.

\subsection{Things the backend deliberately leaves alone}

Several real concerns are not addressed by the Solidity emission
because they are scoped out of the language.  Gas costs as a
strategic factor; mempool-level censorship beyond what the deadline
mechanism handles; cross-contract interactions and oracle reads;
admin keys, upgrade proxies, and pausable patterns.  Each is a deliberate restriction, in the same spirit as the
language's bounded-types-only stance.  Drawing the boundary
tightly is what keeps the analytical backends' outputs faithful
to the deployed code, and what makes a single source for two
views a defensible claim.
