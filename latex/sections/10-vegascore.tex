\section{Connection to VegasCore}
\label{sec:vegascore}

VegasCore is a companion Lean~4 development
\citep{deMouraUllrich2021,mathlib2020} of a failure-aware core
language for executable games with partial information, together
with proofs that its strategic meaning survives compilation to an
event graph and a message-passing runtime.  This
section says what VegasCore is, what it mechanizes, and how the
two artifacts fit together.  Vegas and VegasCore are independent
codebases: the compiler is useful without the library, and the
library is useful without the compiler.  What VegasCore adds is
a mechanized account of when the equilibria of a source game are
equilibria of its deployed protocol, and of the runtime services
that account depends on.

\subsection{What VegasCore is}

The core source language, \TT{SourceProgram}, has four
constructors: \TT{ret} (payoffs), \TT{sample} (public chance from a
declared kernel), \TT{commit} (a binding owned by one player, with
a guard declared at the commitment) and \TT{reveal} (a disclosure
whose result is either the committed value or an explicit
failure).  An initial \TT{Setup} draws the players' private
inputs.  Unsatisfiable guards are admitted: a guard is checked once,
at the reveal that publishes the last value it reads, and is
discharged when any publication it reads failed.  The core compiles
to a dependency-driven \TT{EventGraph} and to a pending-message
runtime with authenticated messages, ideal commitments and relative
deadlines.

\subsection{What VegasCore mechanizes}
\label{sec:vegascore:theorems}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \emph{Outcome and deviation laws.}  Compiling to the event
  graph and the pending-message runtime preserves each compiled
  profile's outcome law exactly, and maps every unilateral
  deviation in the runtime to a finite mixture of legal source
  deviations; hence Nash and same-error $\varepsilon$-Nash
  equilibria are preserved and reflected.  The event-graph proof
  uses a barrier discipline: public events wait for every earlier
  event, and only commitments of different owners are concurrent.
\item \emph{Obstructions to stronger preservation.}  Checked
  counterexamples show that subgame perfection and sequential
  equilibrium are not preserved in general: an early opening, or a
  disclosure that the source records as failed, gives an opponent
  information the source game does not.
\item \emph{Sequential equilibrium under an audited service.}  For
  the full language with finite commitment payloads, every source
  sequential equilibrium has a sequential equilibrium of the audited
  bounded runtime with the same joint law of typed outcome and
  realized settlement.  The service must record authentic evidence,
  classify it soundly, collect a forbidden record with a positive
  probability, and settle a fixed deposit of payoff range over that
  probability.
\end{itemize}

These results fix the service contract a deployment has to meet, and
\S\ref{sec:solidity:audit} describes Vegas's realization of it on
an EVM chain.

\subsection{Where they diverge, and why}

Each artifact has features the other does not.

\paragraph{Surface keywords and macros.}  VegasCore's source
exposes its four constructors directly; Vegas's surface adds the
user-facing \TT{yield}/\TT{join}/\TT{commit}/\TT{reveal}/\TT{random}
keywords, macros, the \TT{or split/burn/null} handler forms, and
the \TT{||} per-query handler.  Handlers correspond to the core's
explicit failure branches; \TT{random} roles, trusted to submit
chance values, have no core counterpart, whose chance construct is
the anonymous \TT{sample}.

\paragraph{The commit-reveal expansion pass.}  Vegas's IR runs
an explicit rewrite (\S\ref{sec:commit-reveal}) that introduces
commit-reveal pairs for concurrent public writes.  VegasCore has
commit and reveal as primitive constructors and never needs a
rewrite.  The rewritten graph is barrier-ordered in VegasCore's
sense: commitments are the only concurrent events, and openings
follow in source order.  A theorem that the rewrite preserves the
strategic content of the pre-rewrite program is not stated.

\paragraph{Quitting.}  In Vegas a player that misses a deadline
quits for the rest of the game; in VegasCore failure is per
disclosure.  The Vegas behaviour is expressible in the core by
guarding each later commitment of the player on the success of its
earlier disclosures.  Both use deadlines relative to readiness.

\paragraph{Private inputs.}  VegasCore's \TT{Setup} gives players
private initial types, as in auctions with private valuations; Vegas
has no such construct yet.

\paragraph{Backends.}  Vegas emits Solidity, Vyper, Gambit,
SMT-LIB, Scribble, MAID, GraphViz, the Diamond DAG witness
records (\S\ref{sec:other:diamond}), and a sketch
Bitcoin/Lightning transcript; VegasCore emits no backends ---
it is a library of definitions and theorems.

\subsection{What this means for using Vegas today}
\label{sec:vegascore:status}

Vegas's design premise is that game-theoretic reasoning about a
program transfers, modulo the stated execution model, to the
deployed contract.  The compiler structures the source so that
this transfer is well-defined: a Vegas program is a game with
public history, information sets given by the visibility
discipline, and a payoff function over the terminal state.  Two
distinct routes are then available for drawing on the transfer.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \emph{Mechanical analysis on the generated artifacts.}  The
  Gambit \TT{.efg} output drives Gambit's equilibrium solvers
  and dominance checks; the SMT-LIB output drives Z3 for
  reachability and coalition queries; the Diamond DAG
  (\S\ref{sec:other:diamond}) supports per-program proofs in
  Coq or Lean.  These artifacts are produced from the same
  EventGraph that drives the deployed Solidity contract.
\item \emph{Reasoning at the source level.}  The Vegas program
  is short enough to read as a game directly --- and for the
  cases where the bundled backends are not the right tool, the
  language is structured to make hand analysis tractable.  The
  conservation property, the visibility discipline, the explicit
  handler choices, and the commit-reveal-canonical IR all bound
  what a strategic reasoner has to consider.
\end{itemize}

VegasCore is a third route, available when the user is willing
to work in Lean: it states when equilibria of a source game survive
deployment, and which runtime services that requires.  A Vegas
program reaches it only through the correspondence of
\S\ref{sec:vegascore}, which is not mechanized.

\subsection{What is and is not proved today}

The current state of the connection:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item Vegas's IR follows VegasCore's semantic choices where the two
  overlap: barrier-ordered openings, deadlines relative to readiness,
  guards discharged by failed publications, and chance that no
  player controls.  This is a design contract, checked by tests
  that replay model traces on chain, not a theorem.
\item The backends agree with Vegas's IR structurally: every
  backend reads the same EventGraph and lowers it
  systematically.  This is a code-review property, not a formal
  one --- there is no compiler proof.
\item There is no formal proof, today, that any specific
  backend's output refines the EventGraph in any externally
  meaningful sense.  The Solidity-to-EventGraph refinement
  proof, in particular, would close the most strategically
  important gap; it is the central new theorem proposed in the
  fellowship-funded continuation of this work.
\item The Diamond DAG (Coq/Lean) backend
  (\S\ref{sec:other:diamond}) is a different route: rather than
  prove correctness of the compiler once, it ships a typed
  certificate \emph{with each compiled program}.  Both routes
  --- verified compiler and verified compiled artifact --- are
  on the project's TODO list as separate workstreams.
\end{itemize}

In short: Vegas's transferability premise is defended today by
three things --- the design of the IR, aligned with the semantics
VegasCore proves results about, the tests that replay the analysis
model's traces on the deployed contract, and the disciplined,
code-reviewed structure of the backends.  A
formally certified end-to-end pipeline is the natural next
milestone, not a prerequisite for present use.
